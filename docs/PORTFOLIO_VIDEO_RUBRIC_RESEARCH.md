# Portfolio Video Rubric Research

Status: Phase 1 research draft  
Scope: rubric grounding only; no existing video was scored and no implementation logic is finalized.

## Research objective

This document grounds a later editorial rubric for selecting social-video work for Ashley Brooke Cohen's website, whose job is to function as an online business card. The governing question is: **Does this video provide useful, legible evidence of who Ashley is, what she brings to the work, what she can do, why she is credible, and why a relevant viewer should want to cast, hire, collaborate with, follow, or trust her?**

The unit of judgment is the complete, retrievable video. A scorer should separate (a) observable properties of the video, (b) audience- and use-case-specific interpretation, and (c) external performance data. Views, follower count, likes, completion rate, and inferred virality may be reported in a separate evidence lane, but they are not proxies for creative quality or portfolio value. YouTube itself treats retention curves as diagnostic signals whose spikes may mean either rewatching or confusion and whose dips may mean skipping or abandonment; these data require interpretation.[^1]

The proposed scales are draft evidence anchors, not automated rules. They intentionally ask what can be seen or heard in the asset. They should later be tested for inter-rater consistency, audience weighting, and corpus effects before implementation.

## Method and evidence labels

Research prioritized sources that directly observe evaluator behavior, validate a construct, publish platform experiments, state buyer criteria, or record named professionals' evaluation practices. Twenty-seven sources were substantively reviewed. Claims below are labeled:

- **Empirical evidence** — peer-reviewed experiments, surveys, field data, or meta-analysis.
- **Platform/industry guidance** — official platform instructions or commissioned buyer research; useful for current practice, but commercially interested.
- **Professional opinion** — named casting, production, or portfolio professionals describing practice; high ecological relevance but not population-representative.
- **Inference** — a transparent application of adjacent evidence to this specific business-card portfolio. Inferences are not presented as established causal findings.

### Source-quality hierarchy

1. Peer-reviewed meta-analysis, experiments, and field studies directly measuring the construct.
2. Official standards, regulators, and transparent industry research with stated sample or method.
3. Official platform research and creative guidance, especially when method or comparison is disclosed.
4. Named, experienced evaluators describing actual casting, buying, or curation decisions.
5. Adjacent academic theory and well-supported inference.
6. Generic advice, listicles, anonymous claims, and vendor marketing. These were not used as primary grounding.

The hierarchy is not absolute. A casting director's account may be more directly applicable to a showreel decision than a laboratory persuasion study, while the study may provide stronger causal evidence. Each metric therefore uses triangulation and records caveats.

## Audience definitions

| Perspective | Decision the portfolio helps them make | Evidence they need from a video | Characteristic risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Agent / Talent Evaluator | Is Ashley representable, positionable, reliable, and capable of generating opportunities across a coherent lane? | A legible identity, repeatable strengths, range without incoherence, professionalism, and a differentiated market position. | Overvaluing broad appeal or current market fashion; confusing one polished clip with repeatable ability. |
| Casting Director / Producer | Can Ashley plausibly serve this role, tone, format, and production environment? | Character choices, specificity, emotional or comedic command, listening/reactivity, on-camera focus, and usable technical presentation. | Role fit is project-specific; a strong video may be irrelevant to a particular brief. |
| Brand / Performance Marketer | Can this creator communicate a product or proposition credibly to the intended audience and support a business objective? | Audience/brand fit, clear value, credible use or demonstration, message control, disclosure readiness, and adaptable native execution. | Platform guidance is mostly ad-focused; portfolio footage is not campaign testing and should not be assigned ROI without data. |
| Creative Collaborator | Would Ashley add ideas, point of view, energy, craft, and dependable contribution to a shared process? | Observable authorship, choices, format fluency, responsiveness to constraints, and evidence that her contribution is more than presence alone. | Collaboration behavior is only weakly observable in finished solo content; avoid inventing off-camera traits. |
| First-Time Audience Member | Can I quickly understand who she is, why this is relevant, and why I should keep watching or trust the claim? | Self-contained premise, comprehensible language and visuals, recognizable point of view, payoff, and accessible audio/text. | Familiar followers possess context that a first-time visitor does not. |
| Portfolio Editor | Does this item improve the set, for a defined audience and purpose? | Strong standalone evidence, distinct contribution, quality floor, current relevance, and low redundancy with stronger items. | Item quality and set contribution are different judgments; a good duplicate can still be a weak addition. |

## Construct definitions

- **Commercial usefulness** is observable evidence that a creator can help a brand communicate a relevant proposition or product experience in a form suited to an intended audience and business objective. It is not estimated sales. IAB buyer research identifies business outcomes as the leading social-video KPI and, in creator selection, emphasizes creator reputation and audience alignment.[^2][^3]
- **Marketability** is not attractiveness, follower count, or a prediction of fame. Here it means that a viewer can identify a specific, communicable casting or collaboration proposition supported by the footage. This operational definition is an inference from work-sample validity, casting practice, and creator-fit research.[^4][^5][^6]
- **Credibility** combines warranted expertise/experience, trustworthiness, accuracy, and transparent presentation. Source-credibility research commonly separates expertise, trustworthiness, and attractiveness, but this rubric deliberately does not score physical attractiveness.[^7] FTC guidance adds truthfulness, actual product use, material-connection disclosure, and clear placement as professional constraints.[^8][^9][^10]
- **Authenticity / natural creator voice** is evidence of internally consistent, specific expression and transparent handling of commercial influence—not rawness, lack of editing, oversharing, or presumed access to Ashley's inner self. Research on influencer authenticity distinguishes passion-led expression from transparent/informational handling of partnerships.[^11] Human-brand work also links perceived authenticity to autonomy and stability, but celebrity evidence may not generalize to a working creator.[^12]
- **Portfolio value** is the marginal evidence an item adds to the complete set for the site's audiences. It depends on quality, relevance, and distinct contribution; it cannot be determined from the item alone.

## Metric-by-metric evidence review

### 1. Identity clarity

**What audiences evaluate.** First-time viewers need to identify the person, subject, stance, and relevance without profile lore. Agents need a position they can describe; collaborators need evidence of authorship rather than a generic trend performance. In short-form platform guidance, TikTok recommends a hook/body/close structure and putting the value proposition early.[^13] YouTube advises that the opening should match the expectation created by the title/thumbnail and hold interest.[^1] This supports early comprehension, not a universal seconds threshold.

**Evidence and application.** Information-gap theory explains why a known question with missing information can create curiosity; total ambiguity is not the same as a productive hook.[^14] Narrative-transportation research likewise requires attention to and understanding of a story world before downstream effects can occur.[^15] The portfolio inference is that a compelling opening can withhold the answer while still revealing what kind of experience is being offered and Ashley's relation to it.

**Caveats.** Platform sources optimize feeds and ads, not business-card portfolios. A deliberately mysterious performance can still prove acting ability. Therefore identity clarity scores legibility of evidence, not literal self-introduction or obligatory on-screen naming.

### 2. Capability proof

**What audiences evaluate.** Casting and hiring audiences prefer demonstrated work to unsupported claims. A work sample is valuable because it resembles the target task, although a meta-analysis found corrected validity around .33—useful, but lower than classic claims and not proof of future performance.[^4] Spotlight frames the showreel as a key discoverability asset, and casting director Kahleen Crawford emphasizes observable character choices rather than expensive technical perfection.[^5]

**Evidence and application.** Capability proof should name the demonstrated action: explains, performs, reacts, improvises, reviews, demonstrates, structures a story, executes transitions, or carries a branded message. The target capability must be sustained enough to distinguish it from a lucky moment. Casting Networks recommends clips where the performer is the focal point, performance is strong, and production quality does not obscure the work.[^6]

**Caveats.** A finished video rarely reveals who wrote, shot, or edited it. Credit only Ashley's observable contribution; mark production authorship unknown when necessary. One sample does not establish reliability across briefs.

### 3. Credibility

**What audiences evaluate.** Brands and audiences evaluate whether the speaker has a basis to know, communicates accurately, and appears candid about incentives. Lou and Yuan found that informational value and influencer credibility contribute to trust in branded content.[^16] Stanford's web-credibility work recommends making claims verifiable and making expertise visible.[^17]

**Evidence and application.** Credit stated experience, firsthand demonstration, concrete details, calibrated claims, visible evidence, attribution, and disclosure when applicable. Product testimony is strongest when the product is actually used and the claim matches what the video shows. FTC guidance says endorsers cannot describe experience they did not have and material connections must be obvious and hard to miss.[^8][^9][^10]

**Caveats.** Confidence, lighting, and fluent delivery can influence perceived credibility but do not establish truth. Prior beliefs can dominate credibility judgments even when evidence quality varies.[^18] Scorers must distinguish warranted credibility from polished authority cues and must flag unverifiable claims rather than infer truth.

### 4. Distinctiveness

**What audiences evaluate.** Agents and editors need a reason to remember and position Ashley; collaborators need a recognizable contribution. Distinctiveness can arise from a specific observation, comic rhythm, lived perspective, framing device, character choice, or combination—not novelty for its own sake. Personal-branding scholarship remains fragmented and lacks universally valid models, so broad “unique brand” formulas are weak evidence.[^19]

**Evidence and application.** Casting director guidance favors explicit character choices, and Spotlight advises choosing material that reflects the performer rather than scenes everyone uses.[^5][^20] Human-brand authenticity research suggests perceived autonomy and consistency matter to authenticity, implying that merely copying a trend may offer weaker identity evidence than adapting it with a recognizable choice.[^12]

**Caveats.** Originality is culturally and corpus dependent. An evaluator cannot claim a format is unique without comparison. Score observable specificity first; assess redundancy and rarity later at corpus level.

### 5. Audience relevance

**What audiences evaluate.** Relevance is fit among content, intended viewer, decision, and context. IAB's creator-economy buyer research reports creator reputation and audience alignment among brands' leading selection criteria.[^3] Experiments comparing influencers with celebrities found identification, similarity, trust, and product-endorser fit affect endorsement response.[^21]

**Evidence and application.** A relevant video makes clear who would benefit, recognize themselves, or use the information/experience; it also supplies evidence needed by at least one portfolio audience. “Broadly relatable” is not automatically better than precise relevance to a casting lane or brand category.

**Caveats.** Fit is dyadic: the same video can be highly relevant to a beauty marketer, moderately relevant to an agent seeking commercial range, and irrelevant to a dramatic producer. Do not collapse these perspectives into a single universal prediction.

### 6. Communication effectiveness

**What audiences evaluate.** Viewers must be able to identify the premise, follow the progression, and recognize the promised payoff. TikTok's official creative framework identifies hook, body, and close; YouTube recommends diagnosing whether openings meet expectations and whether top moments arrive too late.[^1][^13] Narrative transportation research shows that attention, imagery, and affective involvement can influence beliefs and character evaluations.[^15]

**Evidence and application.** Score an opening that frames a legible question/promise, a middle in which each beat advances that promise, and an ending that resolves, demonstrates, reframes, or intentionally lands it. Practical value and high-arousal emotion were associated with sharing in Berger and Milkman's field/experimental work, but sharing is not the same as comprehension or portfolio value.[^22]

**Caveats.** There is no evidence here for one universal hook duration. Comedy, character work, reviews, and tutorials have different payoff structures. A call to action is not required unless the format or commercial objective calls for one.

### 7. Execution quality

**What audiences evaluate.** Technical execution matters when it helps the audience perceive performance and meaning. Casting guidance prioritizes intelligible sound, usable lighting/framing, focus on the performer, and editing that does not delay the strongest evidence.[^6] Meta's Reels guidance recommends vertical video, quality audio, and key messages within the interface-safe zone; TikTok similarly recommends full-screen vertical execution.[^23][^13]

**Evidence and application.** Observe speech intelligibility, distracting noise, exposure, focus, framing, edit continuity, readable/safe-zone text, and whether sound or visual information is essential but unavailable. W3C guidance establishes captions for prerecorded synchronized media with meaningful audio and describes transcripts/visual description as access mechanisms.[^24][^25]

**Caveats.** Lo-fi can be platform-native and credible. Production polish is not a proxy for creative judgment, truth, or acting skill. Penalize only defects that obscure, contradict, or weaken the intended evidence; separately note accessibility gaps.

### 8. Portfolio contribution

**What audiences evaluate.** Editors judge the set, not just isolated clips. Casting Networks recommends a concise reel containing only strongest performances, with the performer focal, contrasting characters when range is useful, and no attempt to include every project.[^6] Spotlight's casting-professional index records the same anti-redundancy principle: do not put everything ever done into one reel.[^20]

**Evidence and application.** A video's marginal contribution can be a new capability, tone, audience, credible domain, character lane, format, or markedly stronger proof of an existing lane. A technically good duplicate contributes little if a stronger video already supplies the same evidence. Conversely, a slightly less polished item may be valuable if it uniquely proves a strategically important capability.

**Caveats.** This dimension cannot be finally scored until the candidate corpus and target mix are known. Item-level review may assign only a provisional contribution based on a maintained coverage matrix. Recency is context, not an automatic quality bonus.

### 9. Commercial usefulness

**What audiences evaluate.** Brand buyers care about audience/creator fit, clear product or proposition communication, reputation, safety, and potential connection to outcomes. IAB reports that business outcomes—sales, site/store action, leads, acquisition—are the most selected social-video KPI, while reach, ad quality, and measurement drive channel selection.[^2] Its 2025 creator report emphasizes reputation and audience alignment in creator selection.[^3]

**Evidence and application.** Credit observable product integration, actual use/demonstration, concrete benefits with support, objections or decision criteria, audience-appropriate language, message hierarchy, safe disclosure placement, and a natural bridge between Ashley's voice and the brand. Online-review research found review depth and extremity affect helpfulness in product-dependent ways, reinforcing that specificity matters but more detail is not uniformly better.[^26]

**Caveats.** A portfolio reviewer cannot infer conversion rate, brand lift, or campaign efficiency from the asset. Sponsored-content and organic-performance evidence must remain separate. FTC compliance is a floor, not a creativity score. Commercial utility is category- and objective-dependent.

### 10. Entertainment / casting usefulness

**What audiences evaluate.** Casting professionals look for choices, character creation, emotional/comedic connection, screen focus, and usable evidence of range. Crawford explicitly prioritizes character choices over technical extravagance.[^5] Casting Networks prioritizes performance quality, emotional connection, screen presence, focal visibility, and contrasting material where relevant.[^6] Research on “thin slices” shows observers form consequential interpersonal judgments from brief behavior, but accuracy is strongest for some affective/interpersonal traits and should not be generalized to employability or character.[^27]

**Evidence and application.** Credit a defined performance proposition, specific behavioral choices, timing, vocal and facial control, responsiveness/listening when visible, comfort with the camera, and an ending/beat that makes the performance memorable. “Marketability” requires a describable lane supported by footage (for example, grounded comic storyteller), not an evaluator's unexamined sense that someone “has it.”

**Caveats.** Casting is role-specific and subject to bias. A creator-address video is not a substitute for a scene, and a polished reel does not prove on-set collaboration. Physical attractiveness is excluded. Personality claims must remain limited to what the video actually manifests.

## Draft observable scoring anchors

Use 0–3 for research review because four levels permit an explicit absence state, partial evidence, usable evidence, and strong evidence. “Not observable” is not automatically zero: when a metric is genuinely inapplicable or authorship cannot be known, record **N/O** and explain. These anchors are not weights or implementation logic.

| Metric | 0 — absent/counterevidence | 1 — partial or fragile evidence | 2 — usable evidence | 3 — strong evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Identity clarity | After full viewing, Ashley's role, subject, or point of view cannot be identified without outside context, or the video attributes a generic premise to no discernible author. | The subject is identifiable, but Ashley's relation or stance emerges late, depends on caption/profile context, or remains interchangeable. | The opening portion establishes the subject and Ashley's role/stance; a first-time viewer can summarize both after one viewing. | The opening establishes subject, Ashley's role, and a specific point of view without outside context, and every major beat reinforces that identity. |
| Capability proof | No target capability is observable, Ashley is incidental, or defects make her contribution impossible to judge. | A capability appears briefly or ambiguously, but authorship, consistency, or task relevance cannot be established. | Ashley visibly performs a relevant task or creative behavior through enough of the video to judge it, with concrete moments that support the claim. | Ashley demonstrates a clearly named capability across multiple beats or constraints, and the finished result makes her contribution and level of control unmistakable. |
| Credibility | Material claims conflict with visible evidence, rely on unsupported certainty, imply use/experience not shown, or hide a relevant commercial connection. | A plausible claim or experience is offered, but its basis is vague, overgeneralized, or difficult to verify; disclosure/evidence is incomplete where relevant. | Claims are calibrated and grounded in visible use, stated experience, concrete detail, or attribution; relevant disclosure is clear. | The video combines a clear basis to know, specific and checkable support, appropriate uncertainty, and transparent incentives while avoiding claims beyond the evidence. |
| Distinctiveness | The item is indistinguishable from a template/trend at the level of wording, choices, and perspective; no Ashley-specific contribution is observable. | One personal detail or stylistic choice appears, but the central treatment remains generic or easily substitutable. | A specific observation, voice, performance choice, or format adaptation makes the piece recognizably Ashley's. | Several mutually reinforcing choices create a memorable, describable proposition that would be difficult to substitute without changing the piece's meaning or effect. |
| Audience relevance | No intended beneficiary/use case is discernible and the item supplies no decision-relevant evidence for a defined portfolio audience. | A likely audience can be inferred, but the language, examples, tone, or proof only loosely addresses its needs. | The intended audience/use case is identifiable and the content supplies information or experience useful to at least one named portfolio perspective. | The piece precisely fits a named audience and decision, anticipates its relevant question or tension, and remains legible to first-time visitors. |
| Communication effectiveness | The premise is not recoverable, major beats do not connect, or the promised question/action receives no payoff. | A premise and ending exist, but setup is delayed/confusing, the middle repeats or digresses, or payoff only partly answers the setup. | The opening frames a comprehensible promise/question, the middle advances it, and the ending delivers a recognizable payoff. | The premise and Ashley's angle are established in the opening seconds, each beat advances or escalates them, and the ending delivers or intentionally reframes the promised payoff without outside context. |
| Execution quality | Speech/essential sound is unintelligible, essential visuals unreadable, Ashley not meaningfully visible, or technical defects prevent evaluation. | Meaning survives, but noise, lighting, focus, framing, pacing, text placement, or missing access support repeatedly obstructs it. | Audio, image, framing, pacing, and text are clear enough that content and performance can be judged without avoidable friction; essential information is available. | Technical and editorial choices actively clarify emphasis, timing, mood, and platform format; Ashley remains focal, text is readable/safe, and essential audio/visual information is accessibly represented. |
| Portfolio contribution | Relative to the current set, the video adds no new evidence and is weaker than an item proving the same lane; or it conflicts with the business-card purpose. | It modestly varies a covered lane or fills a low-priority gap, but duplicates most evidence supplied better elsewhere. | It adds a needed capability, tone, audience, domain, or substantially stronger proof of a current lane. | It fills a strategically important gap or becomes the clearest proof of a core proposition while also complementing, rather than crowding, the rest of the set. |
| Commercial usefulness | No product/proposition or audience benefit is intelligible; Ashley's integration is incidental, misleading, or unusable for a brand context. | A product/proposition appears, but actual use, benefit, audience, message hierarchy, fit, or disclosure is weak/unclear. | Ashley naturally integrates or demonstrates the product/proposition, communicates specific supported value to an identifiable audience, and handles disclosure where needed. | The piece supplies a reusable commercial proof case: natural creator-brand fit, clear demonstration or decision support, disciplined message hierarchy, supported claims, audience fit, and transparent disclosure—without inferring results. |
| Entertainment / casting usefulness | Ashley's performance cannot be evaluated, has no discernible choice, or is obscured by other people/editing/technical failure. | Presence or a character/tone is visible, but choices are generic, inconsistent, too brief, or unsupported by a complete beat. | Ashley is focal and sustains specific vocal, facial, physical, comic, emotional, or presentational choices through a complete beat. | The clip immediately establishes a describable casting proposition and demonstrates controlled, specific choices, timing/reactivity, camera command, and a memorable payoff that adds useful evidence of role or format fit. |

## Perspective-specific review prompts

These prompts preserve audience differences without averaging them:

- **Agent / Talent Evaluator:** What repeatable lane can be described from this footage? Which exact moments prove it? Does the clip expand opportunity while keeping Ashley's positioning coherent?
- **Casting Director / Producer:** What role, tone, and production context does the clip support? Are character/performance choices observable, sustained, and Ashley-focal? What remains unproven?
- **Brand / Performance Marketer:** Who is the audience, what proposition is communicated, what use/demonstration is visible, and why is Ashley a credible fit? Do not predict performance without campaign data.
- **Creative Collaborator:** Which choices plausibly belong to Ashley, and what do they show about idea generation, format command, or craft? Do not infer reliability or teamwork from a solo finished piece.
- **First-Time Audience Member:** After one viewing, can the viewer state who Ashley is in this piece, the premise, her point of view, and the payoff? What context was missing?
- **Portfolio Editor:** What exact evidence cell does this fill? Is there a stronger substitute? What does adding it force the portfolio to repeat, omit, or de-emphasize?

## Known limitations and unresolved questions

1. **No validated composite exists for this use case.** The ten metrics mix communication, persuasion, casting, and portfolio-set judgments. Their convergent/discriminant validity and appropriate weights are unknown.
2. **Audience priorities conflict.** A niche acting clip may score highly for casting and low for a marketer; a review may reverse that pattern. Later implementation should retain perspective profiles or decision rules, not average incompatible judgments.
3. **Corpus dependence is unresolved.** Portfolio contribution requires a coverage matrix and explicit target mix. The number of items, acceptable redundancy, and balance among creator, commercial, entertainment, and credibility evidence remain editorial choices.
4. **Attribution is often unavailable.** Finished social videos do not reliably reveal writing, concept, direction, shooting, or editing ownership. The board needs an “observed / stated / unknown” authorship field.
5. **Platform evidence is commercially interested and ad-heavy.** TikTok, Meta, YouTube, and IAB findings help define current formats and buyer practice but do not validate a personal portfolio rubric. Platform claims should be periodically rechecked.
6. **Casting evidence is ecologically relevant but thin.** Much of it is professional opinion rather than controlled research, and it may reflect UK/US screen-industry conventions. A later calibration round with actual agents, casting professionals, and producers would materially strengthen the anchors.
7. **Authenticity cannot be read directly from style.** Lo-fi aesthetics, eye contact, confidence, or disclosure can be cues but not proof of inner motive. Scores must cite consistency, specificity, firsthand basis, and transparency rather than “felt authentic.”
8. **Marketability is vulnerable to bias.** The rubric excludes physical attractiveness and popularity. Reviewers should document the role/audience proposition and evidence, and audit outcomes for stereotype-based judgments.
9. **Performance remains separate.** If reliable watch-time, retention, conversion, or brand-lift data later exist, display them beside—not inside—the editorial rubric, with sample, platform, time window, and objective.
10. **Accessibility thresholds need a policy decision.** W3C provides a strong standard, but the site must decide whether missing captions or descriptions are an eligibility failure, a remediation task, or an execution deduction before launch.
11. **Context-specific applicability needs rules.** Product demonstration, narrative payoff, disclosure, and casting range are not equally applicable to every format. A later board must define N/O handling without rewarding missing evidence.
12. **Reliability has not been tested.** Before scoring Ashley's videos, use unrelated calibration clips; require evidence citations and rationales; measure disagreement by metric; revise anchors; and reconcile judgments without averaging model opinions.

## Phase 1 handoff

Before implementation, a human reviewer should (1) approve the construct definitions and audience distinctions, (2) decide N/O and accessibility policy, (3) define the target portfolio coverage matrix, and (4) run blind calibration on unrelated videos with evidence-cited rationales. Only after those decisions should the rubric be converted into board prompts or scoring logic. Existing Ashley videos should remain unscored until that review is complete.
